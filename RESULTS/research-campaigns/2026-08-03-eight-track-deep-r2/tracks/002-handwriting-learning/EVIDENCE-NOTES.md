# 002 evidence notes

Evidence was read in full where a full article was available. Page references below refer to the PDF page number printed by the PDF reader, not a search-result position. The notes preserve the distinction between reported result, author interpretation, and this campaign's inference.

## S1 — Shin et al. 2026, EEG experiment

Source: “Taking notes with different writing devices influences learning processes but not performance: an EEG study comparing ink pens, digital pens, and keyboards,” *Frontiers in Education*, 17 April 2026, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2026.1799651. Full PDF, 14 pages, SHA-256 `47d87fba625a6b37a5bf725f77e35f5e2fa47d189d09880a71d139c6c2cd98f`.

- pp. 1–2, abstract: 33 undergraduate participants completed a within-person comparison of ink pen, digital pen, and keyboard note-taking. EEG patterns differed by device and note-taking strategy, while immediate post-tests did not show significant performance differences.
- pp. 3–4, participants/design: 36 recruited and 3 EEG exclusions; final N=33 Korean undergraduates, mean age 22.73. Each participant watched three 10-minute video lectures with ten pseudowords per lecture. Device order was randomized. Five slides used an active/verbatim strategy and five a constructive/question strategy; strategy was counterbalanced.
- pp. 3–4, outcomes: immediate word-recognition and fact-check/comprehension tests followed each lecture. EEG used 32 channels at 1,000 Hz. Repeated-measures ANOVA and FDR correction across 25 electrodes were used, with Bonferroni post-hoc tests.
- pp. 5–7, process result: pens showed higher alpha/beta/gamma activity in reported comparisons, whereas keyboard conditions showed higher theta and theta-beta measures. Several device-by-strategy interactions were reported. Familiarity differed across devices (keyboard mean 3.58; ink 2.94; digital pen 3.42), but adding familiarity covariates did not remove the reported EEG significance.
- pp. 8–12, immediate result: the immediate recognition and fact/comprehension outcomes were not significantly different across devices. The paper therefore supplies evidence for a process difference without an observed immediate-performance difference in this short, artificial task.
- p. 13, limits: lectures were short and used pseudowords; ecological validity and transfer to authentic course assessment are limited. No delayed test was reported. Raw EEG was available on request, so the campaign can audit the article's reported statistics but cannot independently reprocess raw EEG from the public paper.

Interpretation boundary: S1 does not establish that the EEG difference causes a later learning advantage. It also does not test a normal semester course, sustained revision, disability/accessibility, or an external-storage workflow.

## S2 — Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014, original experiments

Source: “The Pen Is Mightier Than the Keyboard: Advantages of Longhand Over Laptop Note Taking,” *Psychological Science*, 2014, DOI 10.1177/0956797614524581. Full PDF, 11 pages, SHA-256 `3f09c120037e305846ae496df226934a5bd0b8524f6b3068e86fcf76cc4a2125`.

- pp. 2–3: the proposed distinction is encoding versus external storage. Laptop transcription and verbatim capture are presented as a possible mechanism, but the paper does not make this mechanism a universal law.
- pp. 3–4, Study 1: final N=65 watched five TED lectures and took a test after about a 30-minute delay. Factual performance was statistically equal (F=.014, p=.91). Conceptual performance favored longhand (F(1,55)=9.99, p=.03, eta-squared=.13). Laptop notes contained more words (309.6 versus 173.4) and more verbatim material (14.6% versus 8.8%).
- pp. 5–6, Study 2: final N=151. An instruction not to transcribe verbatim did not remove the laptop note-volume difference and did not yield a clear laptop advantage. The conceptual result depends on condition and interpretation, not only on device label.
- pp. 7–8, Study 3: final N=109 after return loss; 2x2 design crossed medium with a 10-minute review opportunity. There was no simple main-effect story, but a medium-by-study interaction was reported (F(1,105)=5.63, p=.019, eta-squared=.05). Longhand plus study performed better overall (d=.64); the interaction was stronger for factual performance (d=.97), while the conceptual contrast was marginal (d=.4).

Interpretation boundary: the original paper contains a review-stage interaction and factual/conceptual differences. Its result is not a universal statement that handwriting always beats typing, nor evidence that typing is bad when notes are used as an external memory.

## S3 — Urry et al. 2021, preregistered direct replication

Source: “Taking Notes on a Laptop versus by Hand: Effects on Immediate Learning,” *Psychological Science*, 2021, DOI 10.1177/0956797620965541. Full PDF, 14 pages, SHA-256 `c45d528fcd73613497c897dad0b80c6468dbb85c60cdfa917c1bd6563dce1e70`.

- p. 2, abstract: laptop N=74 and longhand N=68; the replication found no laptop performance advantage. Laptop participants produced more words and verbatim material. An eight-study mini-meta-analysis did not support the immediate-encoding claim.
- p. 3: N=142 analyzed after random assignment; participants watched the same five TED lectures used in the original line of work. The design was powered for approximately d=.47 and used equivalence bounds of plus or minus .49. Materials and data were posted to OSF.
- pp. 6–8: factual achievement g=-.08, 95% CI [-.41, .25]; conceptual achievement g=-.13, 95% CI [-.45, .20]. The intervals were compatible with practical equivalence under the preregistered bounds. Laptop note volume was much larger (g=-.90 for longhand versus laptop coding) and verbatim capture was also larger for laptops (g=-.85 in the reported contrast).
- pp. 8–10, mini-meta: eight studies gave total g=.04, 95% CI [-.13, .20]; factual g=.03 [-.13, .19]; conceptual g=.14 [-.07, .35]. Heterogeneity was low for total quiz performance (I-squared 12.7%) but higher for conceptual performance (38.5%), note word count (69.7%), and verbatim capture (63.6%).

Interpretation boundary: S3 is strong evidence against a large, general immediate encoding advantage for handwriting in its tested lecture-and-quiz setting. It does not resolve delayed learning, revision, external storage, typing skill, or accessibility.

## S4 — Flanigan et al. 2024, meta-analysis

Source: “Typed Versus Handwritten Lecture Notes and College Student Achievement: A Meta-Analysis,” *Educational Psychology Review*, 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10648-024-09914-w. Full PDF, 25 pages, SHA-256 `2e640b43f89fd63dc46e628c1df15037ae78fc4f97804d786444dc36f4dbd9b`.

- p. 1 abstract: 24 studies from 21 articles; achievement favored handwriting with Hedges g=.248, p<.001. Typing produced more note volume with g=.919, p<.001.
- pp. 3–8: the sample included 22 experimental and 2 quasi-experimental studies, 3,005 participants, 49 achievement effect sizes and 15 note-taking effect sizes. There were 29 immediate and 20 delayed achievement effects. Review was absent in 34 effects, optional in 6, and required in 9. Achievement outcomes were coded as total, factual, or conceptual.
- pp. 10 and 12–13: immediate g=.229 [0.146, 0.312] and delayed g=.276 [0.161, 0.392]; timing was not a significant moderator. Review opportunity was associated with a larger handwriting effect: no review g=.208 [0.124, 0.292], optional g=.195 [0.067, 0.323], required g=.421 [0.287, 0.554]. The meta-regression review coefficient was .230, p=.014.
- p. 14: publication-bias tests were not significant, but only seven of 49 achievement effects were unpublished, so those tests do not rule out selective availability.
- pp. 17–21: previous syntheses differed materially (reported estimates include Urry .040 [-.13,.20], Voyer .008 [-.16,.18], Lau .144 [.023,.265], and Allen .250 [.127,.371]). No meta-analysis covered disability needs. The authors identify incomplete reporting of lecture content/images, student characteristics and preferences, motivation, and revision-stage behavior as limits.

Interpretation boundary: S4 supports a small pooled achievement association in the included college lecture studies, not a universal device effect. Review appears important, but the moderator is observational across study conditions and cannot by itself establish that requiring review causes the larger contrast.

## S5 — Negishi & Takeuchi 2026, typing ability

Source: “Effect of Typing Ability on Learning Gain in Typing Note-taking,” *Journal of Japanese Society for Engineering Education*, 74(1), 1_79–1_80, DOI 10.4307/jsee.74.1_79. Full two-page public report, SHA-256 `b9c1dc2bed763ff7b80de5a3dac755921b87accaf0a178f0505b0f68a3c7c42d`.

The accessible report states that a handwriting/stylus/typing experiment found learning gain correlated with individual typing ability in the typing condition but not in handwriting or stylus conditions. The public two-page text does not expose enough detail about sample size, randomization, effect size, assessment timing, covariates, or missing data to reproduce or grade the estimate. It is therefore a hypothesis-generating moderator signal, not a quantitative basis for a general recommendation.

## S6 — Lau 2022, independent quantitative synthesis

Source: Timothy Schaun Lau, “Typed Versus Handwritten Note-Taking: A Meta-Analysis of Learning Outcomes,” University of Louisville dissertation, 2022, 123 pages, https://ir.library.louisville.edu/etd/3982/, SHA-256 `45936a2b81bdddcc0b2aeb9fbf53c5633fbf45e6964f7abe6f6e9bc7abeeec25`.

- Abstract and pp. 23–34: 33 eligible reports, 42 independent samples, and 88 effect sizes; overall handwriting advantage g=.144, 95% CI [.023,.265], p=.021. The dissertation explicitly frames transcription speed, speaker pace, note style, review, intentions, and recall measurement as moderators.
- pp. 23–34: handwriting and typing differ in both transcription capacity and note style; handwriting was described as roughly 5–25 words per minute versus typing 37–77. The dissertation calls the assignment of modality a possible indirect assignment of style, review opportunity, intention, and transcription capacity.
- The synthesis is useful as an independent model of heterogeneity, but it overlaps in primary studies with later meta-analytic work. Its estimate is not an independent replication of all underlying participants.

## Cross-source result map

The sources agree most strongly that typing increases quantity and verbatim capture. They do not agree that this necessarily reduces tested learning. The direct replication and its mini-meta find little immediate difference; the broader meta-analyses find a small pooled handwriting advantage, with review and task design as plausible modifiers. The EEG study adds process differences but no immediate test difference. Thus the evidence is best reported as outcome- and condition-dependent, with a contested small achievement contrast rather than a universal winner.
