##承重 benchmark: Zhang et al.

The full *Science Advances* article and arXiv supplement were read, not a search abstract. The article is Zhang et al., “AI weather models struggle to predict unprecedented extreme weather events,” *Science Advances* (2026). Article PDF SHA-256: `a8608305b038ba5b029e6ff72ab9ec92ec2de99d46e486bef40dd63204f2a86e`; arXiv article-plus-supplement PDF SHA-256: `38bd3350b3a75548103142df902d5c651a175b0e1f4fe6316082b48c3d4ed4aa`.

- Abstract, pp. 0–1: the comparison is ECMWF HRES versus GraphCast, Pangu-Weather, FuXi, and operational variants; HRES is reported to outperform across records, while AI forecasts underpredict record frequency and intensity. The abstract itself says more verification is needed before high-stakes early-warning use.
- pp. 1–2: the record set is defined separately for each variable, grid cell, and calendar month from ERA5 1979–2017. The test years are 2018 and 2020. The reported 2020 ERA5 record counts are heat 162,751, cold 32,991, and wind 53,345 before the paper’s forecast/event comparisons.
- pp. 2–3: on all-data average 2-m-temperature RMSE, most AI models are lower than HRES while Pangu is an exception; on 10-m wind, all AI models are lower. On the record subset, HRES is better at nearly all lead times. The paper also reports the same ordering in the 2018 comparison with a FuXi qualification, and warns that some regions have few or no records.
- p. 2 and pp. 7–9: HRES is evaluated both against ERA5 and in a common operational-style test with HRES-fc0 truth. The paper also reports a forecast-conditioned extreme benchmark intended to address the forecaster’s dilemma; the HRES ordering remains. ERA5 is 0.25° and HRES is 0.1° before the common evaluation grid.
- pp. 3–4: AI forecasts show a growing negative bias as the observed record is more extreme, lower record occurrence/recall, and less favorable precision–recall curves. This is evidence about this benchmark’s tail behavior, not a general theorem about every AI weather model.
- pp. 5–6: the discussion gives interpolation/training-distribution and smoothing explanations, but identifies no causal separation among training data, architecture, resolution, initialization, or post-processing. Probabilistic variants still share the historical ERA5 base, so extrapolation remains an open question.
- pp. 7–9: methods confirm 0.25° ERA5, land-sea mask over 50%, Antarctic exclusion below 60°S, latitude-weighted RMSE, event-subset scores, 1000-bootstrap sensitivity, and strict exceedance of the training-period record. A 31-day running-window alternative gives a similar HRES ordering. Limitations include no precipitation, no in-situ observations, and spatially duplicated representations of one event.

## Independent and competing evidence

1. Olivetti & Messori, *Geoscientific Model Development* 17 (2024), “Can data-driven models beat numerical weather prediction models in predicting extreme events?” Full article PDF was read, SHA-256 `727361937d3fc903b34a414292ed18c204daf73a0dae659ed4d3c74e99996b85` (48 pages). The article compares IFS HRES, Pangu, and GraphCast at a common 1.5° grid against ERA5. Methods (article pp. 6–8) use common HRES initial conditions for operational models and define extremes as the top 1% or 5% of the variable’s distribution, not strict records outside the training history. Results (pp. 10–15) find data-driven models better on average and competitive on many extremes, but regional/event/lead dependence; HRES is often better in high-impact populated regions and in hot/cold tail calibration. This is a genuine competing estimand, not a direct contradiction of the record benchmark.

2. Pasche et al., “Assessing deep learning weather forecasts for extreme events,” arXiv:2404.17652. Full 40-page PDF SHA-256 `13d93fa8c48ea984b215c0f9591586a4395d7bbe219fa24244a1f91e98c428e5` was read. The three case studies are the 2021 Pacific Northwest heatwave, 2023 South Asian humid heatwave, and 2021 North American winter storm. The abstract and results report local comparability in the PNW but aggregate underperformance, substantial underperformance on the compound winter storm, and missing/replaced humidity information for humid heat. The accompanying evaluation repository was read at exact HEAD `b762f9850bf4b835674096c6a2aa306a939ee46f`; it exposes reproducibility notebooks and case-study inputs, not a new independent global sample.

3. WeatherBench 2 official documentation and repository were read. Repository exact HEAD: `95c36d547b22abc2d191451a580b0b194fde67ef`. The official evaluation page exposes public ERA5, HRES, Pangu, operational-Pangu, and FuXi Zarr datasets and their paths. The public metadata were downloaded: ERA5 `.zmetadata` SHA-256 `123bb279f6aa9c6375d5d2c2b95591aeddc797ec7400272a7b38f43f475b35e2`; HRES `.zmetadata` SHA-256 `62811c8101885386acc33e3fe49641af74936d87eb2409ecf47dc193a777db9a`. 4. FastNet physical-guided evidence was read from the technical paper “A physically consistent graph neural network for weather prediction” (arXiv:2509.17601; PDF SHA-256 `d551bdcffe6a2cc14737b72eb56b393f7c56b8f031c480e1296ab387ac6c4720`) and the architecture paper (arXiv:2509.17658; PDF SHA-256 `56c5b3d4a9a26a006655f0e3c51a6092cd920ad32e50d0bc0278826bf5b55280`). 5. ECMWF’s “Machine learning for numerical weather prediction” technical review was read in full, PDF SHA-256 `896efdf79596299e70ab4161e924de9ab2c06986d5d9d4e5c7426b8fa597ac38`. pp. 9–11 discuss unprecedented extremes, models’ inability to extrapolate beyond training ranges, observed-rainfall failures, and the need for holistic verification. ## Public-data reconstruction

The accessible WeatherBench2 ERA5 data were loaded from the official public Zarr. The R2 script reconstructs the strict per-grid, per-calendar-month 1979–2017 threshold and applies it to 2020 at the available 64×32 grid, with `land_sea_mask > 0.5` and latitude > −60°. It yields 349 land-grid record exceedances (2,674 without the land/latitude mask). Month counts are recorded in `reproducibility/output/coarse_record_recalc.json`.

The same threshold is then applied to public 2020 HRES and Pangu forecasts at 24, 48, and 120 hours. The result is a coarse public-data audit, not a replication of the paper’s 0.25° model/version/event table. At 24/48 hours, Pangu’s record-subset RMSE is lower than HRES in this particular WeatherBench2 slice; at 120 hours the two are close (Pangu 2.635 K, HRES 2.626 K). This result is deliberately retained as a competing benchmark observation and cannot be used to erase Zhang et al.’s paper-specific result.

## Evidence boundary

The primary paper supports a bounded statement about a specified record-exceedance benchmark and named model versions. Independent evidence supports context dependence and shows that quantile-defined extremes, case studies, resolution, initialization, region, lead, and truth construction can change the ranking. No source supports the universal claim that AI weather models are either always worse or always better at extremes, and no source supports operational replacement.
